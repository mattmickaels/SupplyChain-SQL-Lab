Supply Chain Data Primer

You already know the planning work and how to read SAP reports. Today is about the layer underneath them: how the data is stored, how to ask it questions in SQL, and how to explain what you found.

Every company, part, customer and number on this page is made up for teaching. The structure matches how a semiconductor equipment maker really plans.

02 · The big picture

Where the data travels

SAP records every business event as it happens. Analysis doesn't run inside SAP. A copy of the data goes to an analytics platform such as Databricks, and the reports you see come from there.

RecordSAPPurchase orders, sales orders, goods receipts, stock moves. One row for every event, as it happens.
CopyNightly extractSAP tables are copied into the lakehouse unchanged. Nothing gets cleaned yet.
ShapeDatabricksData engineers clean the raw copy, join tables and build business-ready tables. Analysts query them with SQL in notebooks.
ReadReports and ExcelDashboards, exports and the spreadsheets you work in every day.

Databricks teams often call those layers bronze (the raw copy), silver (cleaned and joined) and gold (ready for the business). The SQL is the same in every layer, and it's the SQL you'll practice in the lab.

Talking pointWhen a report looks wrong, the cause is usually somewhere along this path: a bad join, a filter on the wrong date, or a field that's blank in SAP. Knowing SQL means you can trace it yourself.
03 · Foundations

Tables, rows and keys

A database table looks like an Excel sheet with stricter rules. Each row is one thing, such as one material or one purchase order line. Each column holds one kind of value, such as a date, a quantity or a code. Nobody merges cells or adds a subtotal row halfway down.

Primary key

The column (or set of columns) that is unique for every row. Material number in the material table. PO number plus line number in the PO line table.

Excel equivalent: the column you'd VLOOKUP on, if you could guarantee it never repeats.

Foreign key

A column that points at another table's primary key. A PO line stores a material number and a supplier number, not the material's description or the supplier's name.

Every join you write follows one of these pointers.

Here's the core of a planning database. Each arrow in your head runs from a FK to the PK it points at.

06 · Reading SQL

How a query reads

Here's a full query that answers a real planning question: which suppliers have the most open quantity on purchase orders that are already past due?

SELECT   s.name,
         COUNT(*)          AS late_lines,
         SUM(p.open_qty)   AS late_qty
FROM     purchase_order_item p
JOIN     supplier s ON s.supplier_no = p.supplier_no   -- the VLOOKUP
WHERE    p.open_qty > 0
  AND    p.delivery_date < CURRENT_DATE                 -- the filter
GROUP BY s.name                                         -- the pivot rows
HAVING   COUNT(*) >= 3                                   -- only repeat offenders
ORDER BY late_qty DESC;

The order you write it

SELECT → FROM → JOIN → WHERE → GROUP BY → HAVING → ORDER BY

The order the database runs it

FROM → JOIN → WHERE → GROUP BY → HAVING → SELECT → ORDER BY

The second order explains most beginner errors. The database gathers the rows first, filters them, groups them, and only then picks the columns. That's why WHERE can't filter on a total, which is what HAVING is for.

08 · Sanity checks
Two traps to spot

      

        

          
Fan-out: numbers that double

          
PO line 4500012/20 ordered 6 pumps and received them in two deliveries of 3. Join the PO line to its goods receipts, and the line appears twice.

          

            
po / lineorder_qtyreceiptrec_qty

            
              
4500012/206GR 13

              
4500012/206GR 23

            
          

          
SUM(order_qty) now says 12. The fix is to total the receipts per line first, then join. Always check: did my row count just change, and should it have?

        

        

          
NULL: blank is not zero

          
A blank lead time in SAP becomes NULL in the database. NULL means "unknown", so it behaves differently from 0.


WHERE lead_wks = NULL      -- finds nothing, ever
WHERE lead_wks IS NULL     -- finds the blanks
AVG(lead_wks)            -- quietly skips blanks

          
An average lead time that skips the missing parts can look much better than reality. Count the NULLs before you trust an average.

        

      

      
Talking pointIn an interview, saying "I'd check the row count before and after the join" signals more experience than writing a clever query.

10 · In the room
Answering a data question

      
Asked something like "Which open customer orders are at risk because of late RF generators?", talk through these steps out loud. The method counts for as much as the query.

      

        
StepWhat you say

        
          
1. Restate"So I'm looking for open sales order lines whose tools need an RF generator that won't arrive in time."

          
2. Tables"Sales order items, the bill of materials, purchase order items, and stock."

          
3. Keys"Sales order to BOM on the tool's material number. BOM to PO lines on the component's material number."

          
4. Filters"Open orders only, RF generator components only, PO delivery date later than the date the build needs the part."

          
5. Check"Before I trust it, I'd compare row counts before and after each join, and look for blank delivery dates."

          
6. Summarize"Seven orders worth about $48M are at risk, five of them from one supplier slipping eight weeks. I'd escalate that supplier first."

        
      

      
The numbers in step 6 are an example. In the lab, the data has problems like this planted in it for you to find.

    


    
    

      
11 · Reference
Glossary

      

        
Table, row, column
A sheet with strict rules: one thing per row, one kind of value per column.

        
Primary key
The column or columns that make each row unique.

        
Foreign key
A column that points at another table's primary key. Joins follow these.

        
JOIN
Combine rows from two tables where their keys match. INNER keeps matches only; LEFT keeps every row from the first table.

        
NULL
An unknown or missing value. It isn't zero and it isn't blank text.

        
Fan-out
When a join multiplies rows and inflates totals.

        
BOM
Bill of materials: what a product is built from, level by level.

        
Lead time
Time from placing a PO to receiving the part. Quoted and actual often differ.

        
Safety stock
A buffer kept on hand to absorb late suppliers or surprise demand.

        
MRP
Material Requirements Planning: works out what to buy and when, from demand, BOMs, stock and lead times.

        
ATP
Available to Promise: what can still be committed to a customer by a given date.

        
S&OP / IBP
The monthly cycle that balances demand and supply; IBP adds financials and a longer horizon.

        
Goods receipt
The record that a delivery physically arrived against a PO.

        
Lakehouse layers
Bronze is the raw copy, silver is cleaned and joined, gold is ready for the business.
